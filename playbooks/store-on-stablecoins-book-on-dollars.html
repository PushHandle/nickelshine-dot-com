1.0

The operating principle: it's your cash drawer

Everything in this playbook follows from one fact: the system is noncustodial. When a customer pays, the funds land directly in a wallet that you — and only you — control. We never hold your money, we never pool it, and we cannot move it. Our software helps you operate; it never stands between you and your funds.

The best way to think about your stablecoin wallet is as a second cash drawer. Like your physical drawer, it takes in payments all day, it pays out refunds on the spot, and it holds a small float so the drawer never runs empty. And like your physical drawer, you periodically deposit the excess at the bank.

The one-sentence model

Operate day-to-day in stablecoins for speed. Keep your books in dollars, always. Sweep to your bank when you need cash there — one button, on your schedule.

One piece of precision that will keep you and your accountant on the same page: a dollar-pegged stablecoin (like USDC) is dollar-denominated — one unit is redeemable for one dollar — but it is not legally identical to a dollar in a bank account. Your bookkeeping treats it as an asset recorded at its dollar value. In practice this changes almost nothing about your daily routine; it just means we'll use accurate language throughout this guide, and so should you.

2.0

How money moves

Ninety percent of what this system does is simple acceptance: a customer pays from their wallet app, funds arrive in your merchant wallet within seconds, and the sale is done. The other ten percent is refunds — full-order or single-item — and those follow the same rule you already know from credit cards: the refund goes back to where the payment came from.

The refund rule, in credit-card terms

With cards, a customer gets their refund on the card they paid with; if they no longer have that card, you fall back to your store policy. Stablecoins work the same way:

  • Customer still controls the paying wallet: send the refund to the originating address. Full or partial amounts both work — refund one item off a basket just as easily as the whole receipt.
  • Customer no longer controls that wallet: the refund is at your discretion, per your posted policy — gift card, store credit, or no refund. Never send funds to a different address on the customer's verbal say-so; that is the stablecoin equivalent of refunding to a different person's credit card.
3.0

The two-balance model: float and bank

Merchants often ask us to convert everything to dollars instantly, because the back office runs in dollars. That's understandable — but converting everything creates a hidden tax: when a refund comes in, you'd pay conversion costs twice (out of stablecoins into dollars, then back into stablecoins to refund). The fix is the same one you already use with physical cash: keep a small float; deposit the rest.

Sizing your float

Start simple and adjust from experience:

  • Starting rule of thumb: hold roughly one to two weeks of expected refunds. If refunds run about 10% of stablecoin sales, that's about 10–20% of a typical week's stablecoin volume.
  • Top-up rule: the float refills itself — each day's incoming payments replenish it before anything is swept. You only set the target level; the sweep takes everything above it.
  • Review monthly: if the float ran dry, raise the target. If it never dips below half, lower it and bank the difference.

Keep the float modest

The float is working capital, not a savings account. Anything beyond what refunds realistically need belongs in your bank, where it can cover payroll and rent. A lean float also keeps your exposure to any single asset small — the same reason you don't keep a month of revenue in the register.

4.0

Sweeps: when to press the button

A sweep converts your stablecoin balance above the float target into dollars and sends it to your bank. It's one button, and you decide when. Here's the key point, and it's worth stating plainly because it's a common misconception:

Sweeping is a cash decision, not a compliance step

You do not need to convert to dollars to close your books. Your books are kept in dollars whether the money sits in the bank or in your wallet — the stablecoin balance is simply recorded as an asset at its dollar value, the same way your inventory is. Sweep when you need cash in the bank; reconcile on your accounting calendar. The two are independent.

So what should drive sweep timing? Two things only:

  • Cash needs. Payroll, rent, and suppliers are paid from your bank account. Sweep far enough ahead that the cash is there when the bills are.
  • Balance discipline. Don't let large balances accumulate in the wallet just because sweeping is optional. A weekly sweep — or an automatic threshold like "sweep everything above float + a set buffer" — keeps balances lean without daily attention.
5.0

The refund procedure

Refunds are executed by you (the owner-operator or an authorized manager) from your own wallet. The procedure is the same for full and partial refunds:

  1. Locate the original transaction in your dashboard — by receipt, time, or amount.
  2. Verify the request. The customer should demonstrate control of the paying wallet (for example, by showing the transaction in their own app), just as you'd ask to see the card for a card refund.
  3. Choose full or partial. For a single item off a basket, enter that item's amount.
  4. Send to the originating address. The system pre-fills it from the original transaction — never type an address by hand.
  5. Record it. Note the refund against the original sale so your ledger nets correctly.
6.0

Bookkeeping and records

Your books stay in dollars, period. What changes is only that you have one more asset account to track. Record, per transaction:

EventWhat to recordWhere it lands
Sale Date, receipt #, dollar amount, transaction reference Revenue; stablecoin asset increases
Refund Original receipt #, amount, destination (origin address or gift card) Revenue reversal; stablecoin asset decreases
Sweep Amount converted, actual dollars received, fee, date Stablecoin asset decreases; bank cash increases

Month-end close checklist

  • Export the month's transactions from your dashboard.
  • Match every refund to its original sale.
  • Confirm wallet balance = float target ± expected variance.
  • Record each sweep at the actual dollars received (fees and any tiny price variance show up here).
  • Hand the export to your bookkeeper — no conversion needed to close.

Two notes for your accountant

Classification: under U.S. GAAP, dollar-pegged stablecoins are generally recorded as an asset at their dollar value — not as cash or a cash equivalent. Your accountant will pick the right account; the balances are identical either way.

Conversions: each sweep is technically a disposal of the asset for tax purposes. On a dollar-pegged stablecoin the gain or loss rounds to zero, but it may still be reportable. This is a bookkeeping line item, not a burden — flag it once and your accountant will handle it routinely. This playbook is operational guidance, not tax or accounting advice.
